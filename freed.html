The Children, The Car, and The Cemetery

Written for: HARC 1029 Photography and the Ethics of Witnessing

Written on January 11, 2026

In Leonard Freed’s 1971 photograph entitled Children Play amongst Rubble and Destroyed Car, Freed utilizes composition and subject placement to tell a powerful narrative of children, war, and death. Rather than showing the violence, Freed depicts it in its aftermath. This shows how destruction and war become normalized in people's lives, especially the lives of the children present in the image.

In this image, we see three core features: the kids, the car, and the cemetery. In the foreground of the image, there are four kids playing in the remains of a boxy coupe car that lies alongside the remains of a house. The car no longer serves its original purpose but is used as a makeshift playground for the kids. This specific transformation of a car is unsettling, as it is an object most would associate with everyday life, but is now a literal hollow shell of itself. There lies wood from the frame, thinner wood from a fence, bricks for the walls, and sheet metal from the former ceiling. What once was someone's home is now a sign of war, and what once was. Destruction has replaced stability in their environment.

The oldest-looking kid stares right into Freed’s camera as if Freed were waiting for the perfect moment to take the picture, and the oldest kid notices Freed there. This is significant because it creates a sort of tension, it feels very confrontational. The child’s stare acknowledges both the photographer's presence and the viewer's role as an observer in a situation that they do not understand. The rest of the kids play, unbothered by Freed or even the destruction around them. This contrast highlights different responses to trauma. To the young children, this is normal as they casually play amongst the rubble, to them, war has become ordinary rather than a large life-altering event. To the oldest boy, he knew a life before war, and that is why he stands staring at and non-verbally confronting Freed and, in turn, the viewers.

The background is the final piece of this narrative, as there is a cemetery littered with crosses, spires, and other forms of grave markers. These various materials frame the children in the image and emphasize. The cemetery sits right behind the children, almost peering over them as a sort of reminder of how close death really is, regardless of age. Freed’s composition of the cemetery within the photograph makes the viewers see childhood and death in the same frame, and not as two separate entities.

In having this juxtaposition of children playing amongst destruction and death, Freed presents a harsh reality. The photograph does not give a resolution or hope, but instead creates a sense of anxiety and shows the endurance of the children. Freed’s use of composition details how war can change one's life and sense of normalcy, turning the remains of a car into a playground and the cemetery into the daily backdrop. Through this brilliant use of composition, Children Play amongst Rubble and Destroyed Car challenges viewers to consider the effects of human conflict, especially in the case of those who grow up knowing nothing else.
